PHASE 5 / VOCAL ENGINEERING

VOCAL ENGINEERING

Design the singer as an engineered performance system: identity, register, delivery, phrasing, harmony, ad-libs, consistency and failure control.

SUNO HOMECREATE GUIDERESEARCH LAB

01 — EVIDENCE RULE

Official V6 evidence: Suno says v6 understands more of the language and building blocks musicians use, including vocals, instrumentation, structure, mood, references and overall feel. V6 also supports plain-language editing of a section, including changing a chorus to a different vocal concept. That establishes vocal direction as a legitimate input surface; it does not establish that every descriptive adjective behaves like a deterministic control.

Research rule: treat exact vocal descriptors, bracket annotations and negative prompts as conditioning experiments unless Suno documents a specific behavior.

02 — VOCAL IDENTITY

Start with identity before ornamentation. Define gender presentation, age impression, register, vocal character, delivery and emotional stance in a compact order.

female lead, adult, low-mid register, warm chest voice, intimate close-mic delivery, restrained emotion, clear diction

Do not stack contradictory identities. Test one identity change at a time when comparing generations.

03 — REGISTER + TESSITURA

Register describes the perceived operating area of the voice. Tessitura describes where most of the song sits rather than its single highest note.

lower-register verses → rising upper-mid pre-chorus → strong mixed-register chorus → lower intimate outro

Use song-section language instead of assuming a single global descriptor will force every note into one register.

04 — CHEST / HEAD / MIX LANGUAGE

  • Chest: grounded, speech-like, weighty lower/mid delivery.
  • Head: lighter, elevated, floating upper-register character.
  • Mix: powerful upper-mid delivery without explicitly demanding a full chest push.
[Verse — grounded chest voice, conversational]
...
[Chorus — strong mixed register, open vowels]
...
[Bridge — light head voice, intimate]
...

These are performance descriptions, not guaranteed vocal-engine commands.

05 — BREATHINESS, RASP, GRIT

Separate texture from intensity. Breathy does not necessarily mean quiet; raspy/gritty does not necessarily mean shouted.

breathy close vocal, controlled air, clear pitch, no whispering
clean lead with restrained edge, subtle grit only on sustained climactic words

For clean-vocal testing, explicitly exclude unwanted textures and compare repeated generations.

06 — ARTICULATION

Specify whether consonants should feel crisp, soft, conversational, clipped, legato or deliberately blurred.

clear consonants, intelligible lyrics, natural connected phrasing, no exaggerated diction

Lyric geometry from Phase 4 interacts strongly with articulation: syllable density and line length can change how much space the vocal has.

07 — VIBRATO / MELISMA / SUSTAIN

These are useful as behavioral descriptions rather than guaranteed switches.

mostly straight tone, subtle vibrato only at phrase endings
restrained melisma, short tasteful runs, sustained final vowel on the hook

Test one ornament at a time. If adding three ornaments changes the entire melody, you cannot identify which variable caused it.

09 — BACKING VOCALS + HARMONY DENSITY

Specify who sings, when, and how densely.

single lead in verses; sparse low harmony on chorus tails; three-part harmony only on the final chorus hook; no continuous choir

“Harmony” and “choir” are not interchangeable. If you want a solo lead with occasional doubles, say so explicitly.

10 — CALL AND RESPONSE

[Chorus]
Lead: I still hear your name
Backing response: your name
Lead: I still hear your name
Backing response: don't let go

Test whether explicit role labels help, then compare against ordinary lyrics with the same words. Keep successful notation patterns as experimental recipes rather than universal rules.

11 — VOCAL PLACEMENT

Placement is best described perceptually:

  • forward / present
  • close / intimate
  • centered lead
  • wide backing layers
  • distant / atmospheric supporting vocal
centered present lead vocal, intelligible and forward; backing layers wider and quieter; lead remains dominant through the climax

12 — CONSISTENCY ACROSS THE SONG

When vocal identity drifts, test whether the drift is tied to section, duration, arrangement density or generation randomness.

consistent lead vocal identity throughout, stable timbre, stable vocal character, controlled intensity progression, no unintended late-song vocal transformation

Community reports during the first V6 rollout include both strong vocal results and complaints about muffling, over-processing and late-song degradation. These reports are useful signals, not proof of a universal defect.

13 — LATE-SONG VOCAL DEGRADATION

Build a diagnostic pair:

CONTROL: same prompt/settings, normal full song
TEST: same prompt/settings, shorter song or isolated late section workflow

Compare clarity, sibilance, pitch stability, reverb, loudness, distortion, vocal masking and arrangement density. If the problem appears only late, test section editing or replacement rather than assuming the vocal prompt is the root cause.

14 — AD-LIB CONTROL

lead remains lyrical and focused; sparse improvised ad-libs only between lines; no constant ad-libbing

For a tightly controlled lead, explicitly state the desired ad-lib density. Then test an exclusion such as no constant ad-libs, no random vocal fills.

15 — AVOIDING UNWANTED CHOIR

single lead vocalist, sparse optional backing harmony only on selected hook endings, no choir, no ensemble lead, no massed vocals

Compare this against a positive-only prompt. If the result still produces a choir, record the failure rather than endlessly adding negative words.

16 — AVOIDING UNWANTED DOUBLES

one clearly dominant lead vocal, minimal doubling, no constant unison double, backing vocals only where specified

Again: a negative phrase is a test variable, not a guaranteed suppression mechanism.

17 — VOCAL FAILURE TAXONOMY

  • Muffled: lead lacks presence or intelligibility.
  • Buried: instruments mask the lead.
  • Over-processed: audible artificial modulation, compression or texture.
  • Drift: timbre or delivery changes unexpectedly.
  • Over-layered: doubles/harmonies accumulate.
  • Wrong register: generated tessitura misses the target.
  • Wrong delivery: whispered/intimate request becomes full-power singing, or vice versa.
  • Late-song collapse: vocal quality changes as the arrangement develops.

19 — THE VOCAL ENGINEERING RULE

Identity → register → delivery → texture → phrasing → harmony → placement → constraints. Start simple. Add one variable. Repeat. When a result survives multiple generations, promote it from “interesting” to “repeatable.”
